Making a Realistic Trailer From a 3D One

Bryan Falkowski · November 18, 2026 · About the Building58 trailers · Follows Making a Short Film From a Short Story

Building58 is a building of 3D rooms where my Claude Code sessions work. Its first trailer is 79 seconds of one room at work, on a break and at a party, with its own 80s music. Everything in it is 3D. I wanted the same trailer with real-looking people in a real loft.

Three days earlier I made a short film of Doctor Bones, and it used almost all of the 3,000 credits in my Higgsfield subscription. So this time the first question was cost. Claude priced two plans before anything was made, and the cheaper plan made the trailer for about 250 credits. It took one morning and six cuts. This post explains the plan, the cast, the takes, my rounds of notes and the numbers. Every cast prompt is in a box you can open, and so are the prompts for the room and the main shots.

What changed in the tools

The work split the same way as in the short film. Higgsfield made the material. Claude planned the shots, wrote the prompts, spent the credits and did the editing. I directed. Two things were different.

First, I did not use the Higgsfield CLI. I added Higgsfield as a connector in the Claude app. A connector lets Claude call a service's tools directly, so Claude could make an image, start a video, wait for it and check my credit balance without a terminal.

Second, the editing ran on Higgsfield's computer, not on Claude's. The connector comes with a sandbox, which is a Linux machine in Higgsfield's cloud with ffmpeg and Python. Claude's own workspace could not download files from Higgsfield, so every take, frame and sound stayed in the sandbox, and Claude did all of the cutting and mixing there. That is also why the finished video plays from a Higgsfield link and not from my site.

Claude still cannot watch a video. It checks a cut with still frames, a scan for sudden jumps between frames, sound levels and speech-to-text. I watched every cut and wrote the notes.

Two plans, priced first

The first trailer was made in the 3D office, so it already worked as a storyboard. Claude broke it into 24 shots and built a storyboard page in the same format as the Doctor Bones one. It also made an animatic: the 3D frames as stills, timed to the cut, with the trailer's own music. Then I asked about cost. My note, typos fixed:

The lesson from Higgsfield is that it was expensive. What can we do to make sure the cost is lowered substantially?

I wanted to keep the details of the expensive plan in case we went back to it, so the storyboard has two versions that you can switch between:

  • v1, full video. Every shot with a camera move is a generated video on Seedance 2.0. That is 71 seconds of video, about 320 credits for one try.
  • v2, lower cost. Only shots where people move are generated. That is 41 seconds of video, about 130 credits for one try.

I picked v2. These rules kept its cost down:

  • Generate only what moves. The window, the neon sign, the two computer screens, the day passing and the pull-back at the end were planned as still images, with a slow camera move added in the edit. The title cards and the end card are cut from the 3D trailer itself.
  • Make long takes and cut them in the edit. This was the lesson from the short film. One take over the desks gives the room at work and, later, the manager walking through. One 13-second orbit around the party gives four shots and three photos.
  • Use a cheaper model where it is enough. Seedance 2.0 at 720p costs 4.5 credits a second, and it can take the cast sheets as references. Kling 3.0 costs 1.75 credits a second. Seedance made only the two crowd takes. Kling made the shots with one or two people.
  • Keep the music. The trailer's music is written to this cut, so it is used as it is. Nothing was generated for music.
  • No lip sync. The plan had one line of dialog, heard off-camera.
  • Set a cap. Each take gets two tries at most. After that, Claude stops and asks before it spends more.

Here is the storyboard as it stood after the first cut. The buttons at the top switch between v1 and v2. Some of it changed later. For example, the party still celebrates a merged pull request, and the day passing is still a set of stills.

The cast

The 3D office already has a roster of workers, each with a name, a job and a look. For the real people, this is all the direction I gave, typos fixed:

We could use the hacker as a minor character, since we have the sheet for that already. The rest of the office are just regular awkward nerdy types trying to act cool. You know, software developers.

The hacker is the young man from Doctor Bones. He sits on the couch at the party. Claude wrote one prompt for each of the other nine people, using the roster's names, jobs and looks. Each prompt follows the short format of the young man's prompt from the short film: who the person is, one line about how they try to look, the clothes, and then the same block for the layout and the look of the sheet. GPT Image made each sheet for 1 credit, so the whole cast cost 9 credits.

GPT Image made the first frames from three kinds of reference: the 3D frame for the camera angle, the cast sheets for the people, and one master image of the room. The master image is the whole loft at night from a high corner. Every other frame uses it, so the rug, the couch, the espresso machine and the sign stay the same in every shot.

The first frames also cost 1 credit each, 15 in all with one retry. The retry was the party, because the first try left out Marcus. The second prompt says where each person stands. I looked at the frames before any video was made.

One test before spending

Before the takes, Claude made the same shot twice from the same first frame: Paul at his desk for 4 seconds, once with Kling 3.0 and once with Seedance 2.0. They looked the same. Kling cost 7 credits and came out at 1080p. Seedance cost 18 credits at 720p. So the four small takes went to Kling.

The takes

The first cut used seven takes, which cost 147.5 credits with the test:

  • Take A, 8 seconds on Seedance 2.0. The camera drifts over the desk rows, and the manager walks in partway through. It gives the room at work and, later, the manager's walk.
  • Takes C to F, 4 seconds each on Kling 3.0. Marcus at his desk, Noor at the espresso machine, Sam at the window and the ping-pong game.
  • Take G, 13 seconds on Seedance 2.0. One slow orbit around the party that ends close on the cake. The edit cuts it into four shots, and three times the frame freezes into a tilted photo with a white border, a caption and a camera shutter sound.
  • Take B, the Kling test of Paul.

The models did not always follow the prompt. In take A the manager walked in at about 1.5 seconds instead of 4. The edit slows down the first part and crops it to keep him out of the room at work, and it uses the later part for his walk. Take G starts wider than its first frame, and the cake ends up with more candles than it started with. Higgsfield also refused take G once with an out-of-credits message while I still had credits. The same request went through on the second try.

The screens

Priya's and Theo's monitors are still images of a real monitor, with the text added in the edit. In my notes on the early cuts I asked for random terminal output. Claude wrote a script that types out a real-looking session line by line. On Priya's screen, Claude Code reads files, shows a diff and runs the tests, and the run ends with 84 passing tests. Theo's screen runs git, pushes a branch and opens a pull request. The script draws the text flat, then bends it in perspective to sit on the monitor glass, with a blinking cursor and a soft glow. Because it is made in the edit, the text costs no credits and is exactly what Claude wrote.

Rounds of notes

The trailer went through six cuts. The first cut put the takes and the stills together, and the second added the sound. I liked both. After that, each round started with me watching the new cut and writing notes. My notes, typos fixed, and what changed:

  1. Cut 2 to cut 3.

    There are some editing issues where it seems the storyboards flicker. The "Let's ship it" doesn't really work, and the text on the screens should be random terminal output.

    The flicker came from the title cards. They are cut from the 3D trailer, and at each fade a frame or two of the 3D office showed through. The edit now holds black on those frames. Paul's line had been an off-camera voice over Priya's screen. Claude made a new 4-second Kling take with sound, where Paul looks up and says it, for 10 credits. The screens got the terminal script.

  2. Cut 3 to cut 4.

    When she says "Cake!" it sounds strange. Remove that. Also, the text on the second scene with the city backdrop is too small. Then the last scenes, where we originally had time-lapse video. Let's generate that. I liked it more.

    June's line is gone, and the text over the window is bigger. The day passing and the pull-back became generated takes on Kling, 5 and 7 seconds, for 21 credits. Kling can take both a first frame and a last frame, so the day passing starts on the loft by day and ends on the master image at night.

  3. Cut 4 to cut 5.

    The celebration at 0:47 has "PR #16 merged." Can we have a better reason for celebration? At 1:03 to 1:04 a person at their desk glitches, and I would have liked to see more movement in the office. The office sounds between 0:22 and 0:30 are too loud. And finally, the manager's movement between 1:01 and 1:02 seems to jump a little. That can be smoother.

    Claude gave me four reasons to choose from, and I picked one about Autopilot, which is how my office fixes and ships its own code. The photos now say "Autopilot shipped it overnight," "Nobody touched a keyboard" and "Friday cake." The manager's two shots became one continuous piece of take A, so he no longer jumps. The typing is 9 dB quieter. The day passing became a new 9-second Kling take with a lot of movement, sped up about two times in the edit.

  4. Cut 5 to cut 6.

    The time-lapse started great, but then had way too many people.

    I asked for more movement, and the prompt for the second try asked for many people walking the whole time. Kling took it literally and filled the room. The third prompt gives a number: about eight people, never more than ten. That take is in the final cut.

Sound

The music is the 3D trailer's own track. Claude made nine sound effects with a sound effects model on Higgsfield, such as city noise through the window, the neon hum, typing, the espresso machine, ping-pong, the party, a camera shutter and the room tone. Together they cost about 16 credits. ElevenLabs made four test lines for about a quarter of a credit each. None of them is in the final cut, because Paul's line comes from his own take.

Claude mixed everything in Python. Each sound has its own level, the music and effects drop under Paul's line, and the loudest peaks are limited. It checked each mix with loudness measurements and with speech-to-text, which showed that Paul's line is clear.

What I learned

  • Price the plan before you make anything. Two versions side by side made the trade clear, and the storyboard kept the expensive version in case I wanted it later.
  • A still with a slow move is enough when nothing moves. The window, the sign and the screens are stills, and none of my notes asked for them to move. The day passing is the opposite. Its whole point is movement, and I liked it better as video. It became video, and it took three tries.
  • Use the expensive model only where it does something the cheap one cannot. Seedance made the two crowd takes, where the cast sheets kept everyone the same. Kling made the rest for less than half the price a second.
  • Most fixes are free. The flicker, the screens, the photo captions, the manager's jump and the sound levels were all changes to the edit. Only the new generations cost credits, and they came to 62.5, a quarter of the total.
  • Give the model a number. "Lots of movement" made a crowd. "About eight people, never more than ten" made an office.
