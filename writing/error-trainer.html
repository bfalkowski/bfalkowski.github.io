Is the Reviewer Getting Better? Design Notes for an Error Analysis Trainer

Bryan Falkowski · October 18, 2026 · Design notes · Try it · Code

Second Look is a small app that teaches people to review LLM traces and measures whether they actually get better at it. The learner labels a support conversation pass or fail, sees the reference label, and gets coached on the ones they miss.

The main design decision is how improvement is measured. Accuracy while the coach is helping measures the tool, not the person. So the learner is scored on conversations they haven't seen, with no coaching, before and after practice, using the same TPR and TNR I use for LLM judges.

It also works with the Bluetooth remotes people use to flip Anki cards, with notes spoken instead of typed, and that may turn out to be the best way to review.

Why build this

Error analysis means reading real traces, writing down what went wrong, and grouping the failures into categories you can count and fix. It is the step that tells you which evals to write, and it is the one most teams skip or rush.

The conversations in this app are an extremely simplified version of the proprietary traces I work with: a fictional internet provider, ten short support rules, and one planted failure per conversation. Real traces are longer and messier, but the skill is the same. You get better at it by labeling traces and finding out which ones you got wrong, and that feedback usually depends on someone more experienced checking your labels.

A tool can give feedback on every label. The risk is that it ends up doing the reviewing for the learner. If the coach points at the failure every time, the learner's numbers go up inside the app and nothing carries over to their own traces. Most of the design below is about avoiding that.

The loop

A session has three phases. Only the first and last are used to measure skill.

10 new traces, no coaching]

Each practice item works like this:

  1. Read and label. The learner reads one conversation, marks it pass or fail, and writes a one-line note on what went wrong. The note is required for a fail, because naming the failure is the actual skill.
  2. Reveal. The learner sees whether their label matched the reference.
  3. Coach on misses. On a miss, the coach asks a question that points at the evidence, for example "look at turn 4, what did the customer ask for?", and the learner writes a second look before anything is explained.
  4. Explain. The reference note, the failure type and an explanation appear next to the learner's own note.

One thing changed once I started building. With only two labels, telling someone they missed also tells them the answer, so a second look can't be a second guess at the label. It's a second try at naming what actually happened, which is the harder half of the skill anyway.

Another changed after trying it. Ten unscored conversations before any feedback is a lot to ask of someone who just wants to practice, so the app now starts two ways. Practice begins coached rounds right away and can stop after any round. Its results show first looks round by round and say plainly that this doesn't measure improvement, since nothing was scored beforehand. Measuring yourself keeps the pre-check, practice and post-check, and can also stop after any round.

Reviewing with an Anki remote

Second Look works with the small Bluetooth remotes people use to flip Anki cards. Most of them act as a keyboard, so the app asks you to press the button you want for Pass, Fail and Next, and from then on those three buttons run the whole session: label, submit, move past the hint and the explanation, and start the next round.

This may be the better interface for review. You sit back and read the conversation instead of hovering over a keyboard, and each decision is one press, the same rhythm Anki users already know. Most of the work in error analysis is reading, and the remote keeps the hands out of the way.

The obvious gap is notes. A fail should come with a note on what went wrong, and a remote can't type. So pressing Fail on the remote also turns on the microphone. You say what went wrong, the words show up in the note as you talk, and Next submits it. Pressing Fail again stops listening. The second look after a hint works the same way. If you press Next on a fail without saying anything, the app asks once before moving on, so a fail with no note is a choice and not an accident.

The speech recognition comes from the browser. Chrome, Edge and Safari support it and Firefox doesn't. Chrome does the recognition by sending the audio to Google, and Second Look keeps only the text. That's fine for these practice conversations. For real traces it's worth knowing before you read a customer's account details out loud, and on-device recognition would be the better choice.

Fading the help

The help on a miss depends on the learner's last four first looks:

Recent first-look accuracyHelp on a miss
Below 50%, or fewer than two itemsA question that points at the key turn, then a second look
50% to 74%"Take another look", with no pointer, then a second look
75% or moreNo hint; the explanation comes right away

The coach should make itself less necessary as the learner improves. If a learner keeps getting full hints round after round, the practice isn't working.

What "better" means

The learner is acting as a judge, so I measure them the way I measure an LLM judge (see the TPR and TNR primer). Against the reference labels:

  • TPR: of the conversations that should pass, the share the learner passed.
  • TNR: of the conversations that should fail, the share the learner failed. I expect this to be the weak number for most new reviewers, for the same reason it was weak for the judge in the primer: failures that look fine on the surface get passed.

The app records several numbers, and only some of them measure skill:

MeasurementWhenWhat it tells you
Second look found itPractice, after a hint, self-reportedWhether the hint helped. Not skill.
First-look TPR and TNRPractice, before any hintProgress on this practice set. Useful feedback for the learner, but inflated by learning the set itself.
Pre-check TPR and TNRBefore practice, no coachingStarting skill
Post-check TPR and TNRAfter practice, no coachingSkill the learner keeps without help, on conversations they have not seen
Note matchCheck-set conversations the learner failedWhether the learner named the right failure, not only the right label

Only the change from pre-check to post-check counts as improvement.

The traces

  • Source. 44 conversations with the support agent for Larkhill Fiber, a fictional internet provider with ten support rules. Every customer, address and account is made up.
  • Seeded failures. Each failing conversation has exactly one planted failure, from five types: a wrong policy stated with confidence, part of the customer's question ignored, a tool problem the agent hides, a polite reply that resolves nothing, and a required check or disclosure skipped.
  • Passes that look bad. Some passing conversations are built to be failed by a careless reviewer: an agent turning down a credit the rules don't allow, a tool error the agent reports honestly, a refusal to share a balance before verification. These are what TPR catches.
  • Reference material. Each conversation has a reference label, a one-line note, the key turn, a scripted hint and an explanation, all written before any learner sees it.
  • Splits. The pre-check and post-check have 10 conversations each, 5 pass and 5 fail, with one of each failure type. Practice has 24, in three rounds of 8 with 4 of each label. No conversation appears in two sets.

Checking the coach

The coach is Claude when an API key is configured, and a scripted coach otherwise. Claude is an LLM, so it needs its own evals:

RuleHow it's checked
A hint never gives the answer awayNo verdict words (correct, wrong, mistake) and none of the trace's giveaway terms, which are written per trace
A full hint points at the evidenceIt names the key turn by number and ends with a question
The explanation comes only after the second lookOrder of events in the session log
Help fades as the learner improvesThe logged help level matches the policy above, recomputed from the logged first looks

The first two rules run on every hint Claude writes, before the learner sees it. A hint that breaks one is replaced by the scripted hint, and the broken rule goes into the session log. The same checks run on all 44 scripted hints in the dataset tests, which caught six scripted hints that weren't questions. All four rules run over a finished session with a small report script.

Interaction details

A labeling tool gets used for long stretches, so speed and clear feedback matter more than decoration.

  • Keyboard first. P and F to label, Enter to submit, K and J to move back and forward, R for the rules.
  • Undo in the check sets. A slip on P or F can be taken back until the results appear, with the note put back in the form.
  • References are links. "turn 7" or "R3" in the coach's text scrolls to that turn or opens that rule.
  • The reveal. The learner's label and the reference sit side by side, then the learner's note next to the reference note.
  • Follow the hint. When the coach points at a turn, the conversation scrolls to it and a highlighter mark sweeps across it.
  • Live rates. A small meter shows first-look TPR and TNR as each label lands, so the learner can see which kind of mistake they make.

Stack

React, TypeScript and Vite on the front end, with Motion for transitions. A small FastAPI service calls the Claude API (Haiku 5.5 by default) for hints, explanations and note grading, and stores session events in SQLite. The conversations, reference labels and coaching rules live in the repo, and the front end and backend read the same file. The demo runs the front end alone with the scripted coach.

Left out of v1

  • Accounts and multiple users. A session lives in one browser.
  • Grouping notes into categories. This is the second half of error analysis (axial coding) and deserves its own pass once labeling works.
  • Disagreeing with the reference. Some traces are ambiguous enough that reasonable reviewers disagree. There's no way yet to flag a reference label, and flagged traces should be reviewed rather than counted as learner errors.

Next step: your own rules and traces

The next version lets a team upload its own rules and its own traces, so people practice on the product they actually review. That changes what has to come with the upload:

  • Practice needs a reference for each trace. At minimum a label and a one-line note from someone the team trusts. The coach can write the pointer question and the explanation from those, and the same guardrail checks it.
  • Measuring needs held-out check sets. A pre-check and post-check drawn from the uploaded traces, balanced between pass and fail and never used in practice.
  • The reference labels are now the weak point. In the built-in dataset every label was written and checked up front. With a team's traces, a wrong reference teaches the wrong lesson, so labels should come from two reviewers, with disagreements settled before anyone practices on them.

Open questions

These are the things the current version can't answer yet.

  • Are 5 conversations per check enough? Not for one person. If you catch 3 of the 5 failing conversations, your real rate could be anywhere from about 23% to 88%. So one learner's improvement only shows up if it's large. Bigger check sets, or combining results across many learners, would fix that.
  • Is it teaching the skill, or just these five failure types? Practice and the checks use different conversations but the same five kinds of failure. A better post-check shows you can spot those five in new conversations. It doesn't show you'd catch a kind of failure you've never seen. A check set with new failure types would test that.
  • Can Claude grade the notes? Right now you grade your own notes against the reference, and Claude's grades are recorded next to yours. Before Claude's grades count on their own, they need to be checked against a person's grades, the same way an LLM judge is checked in the primer.
  • Are spoken notes as good as typed ones? Talking is faster, but spoken notes tend to run longer and looser. They might name the failure less precisely, or they might capture more of what the reviewer noticed. Comparing note grades between remote and keyboard sessions would show which.
  • Does it carry over to real work? The real test is whether someone reviews their own product's traces better afterward. That needs a follow-up on real traces, labeled by a second reviewer.
